Fatma literally says when asked if she’s scared to go out at night when she can hear helicopters over the town: "We put our soul in our hands and walk."


Lately, I’ve been focusing mainly on documentaries where I hoped to find hope and beauty—partly as inspiration for a documentary I’d like to make myself. At the film festival in Sneek, there were only three documentaries on the program, and I wanted to see them all. Moreover, I wanted to see something about Gaza.
Fatma is a young female photographer from Palestine who captured the war there and its consequences. She came onto the radar of filmmaker Sepideh Farsi, who approached her to create a personal eyewitness account of Fatma’s life through video calls. I was particularly struck by Fatma’s hope and future plans, despite being trapped with her family in a desperate situation of continuous bombardment. It also struck me that Fatma almost always appeared on screen beautifully made up.

How did I leave the theater? Silent. Impressed. I realize how good we have it here in the Netherlands. That you have to make every day a beautiful one. That it is terrible how people who have no part in conflicts, and who simply live peacefully with their surroundings, are forced to endure such hardship just because they happen to live where an aggressor mows down everyone in their way. It’s bizarre that as spectators, we see so much and yet remain so powerless. At least, I feel there is nothing I can do. Nothing other than writing an article about the film I saw. It’s so strange in this age of social media, where large groups could easily organize via the internet you't think. Yet, those with the best weapons have the final say right now it seems. Life ain't fair. 

## Tension
At first, I wondered if I could stay focused for nearly two hours just watching talking heads on phone screens. I did so effortlessly; I sat through the entire film breathless. For me, that’s a rarity. It was edited in such a way that I was captivated from start to finish. The inherent tension, beautifully built up, was that every time Sepideh called, you wondered if Fatma would pick up. Every ringtone was a cliffhanger.

## Reality
You don’t just see talking heads; occasionally there are other images—the photos Fatma took. Through what she tells us about her family's life, their experiences, and their surroundings, you receive new information throughout the film. The story grows richer and richer.

It is, of course, the most personal story about a war you can get. Fatma was so brave, accepting her reality with a smile, although she grew somber toward the end.

There were moments that truly demanded your full attention. For instance, when they spoke about bombing and the screen suddenly went black, leaving only the sound of an explosion. It startled me; it really hit home. It was a powerful realization that you are watching people being bombed almost in real-time.

The ending—the camera "driving" through the streets accompanied by Fatma’s voice is heartbreaking. She and her family were killed.

## Ethics
The contrast between the filmmaker in a safe world and the protagonist in a life-threatening situation made me reflect on the ethics of the maker. What is your responsibility when showing someone in mortal danger—someone who eventually perished in the war?

Sepideh hinted a few times at Fatma leaving Gaza. I suspect she wanted to help. But Fatma initially wanted to stay, and by the time she did want to leave, her situation seemed hopeless: nowhere to go. It’s moving and gut-wrenching when, during one of the final calls, the filmmaker shares that the film was selected for Cannes and asks if she’ll come. Fatma says she will try. Shortly after, the family is shot dead.

## The Form
There isn’t much choice when making a film based on video calls; you film the phone. Perhaps it’s strange, but the first thing I noticed in the opening shots was that the phone was on "airplane mode," yet they were video calling. I wondered if the video feed was edited in afterward, though that seems unlikely. Later, the phone was never on airplane mode again.

Every time the filmmaker called, I held my breath, hoping Fatma would answer. That is, of course, "driving the story forward": building tension even though you know how it ends.
The audio was good; I’m curious how it was recorded. The setup seems simple: the filmmaker has a phone on a small tripod and films the conversations with a separate camera. You see her phone in various locations. Since Sepideh Farsi travels frequently, I suspect she uses a DSLR, perhaps with 8K footage to allow for cropping in post-production. I hope to find some "behind the scenes" footage someday.
There is a moment where Fatma reads her poem. Her voice is layered over photos she took of her environment. Because I have to read the subtitles, I don't always see what's in the photos and vice versa.

## Enough
On one hand, I think it wasn't technically difficult to make, film, and edit this. On the other hand, I think the filmmaker must have had months of agonizing anxiety. And what I also learned from Fatma: I feel you standing next to me, you talk to me, you stand by me. That is enough.
